# Recommendations — the football transfer market

**For:** the investment committee of a multi-club fund deciding where its next tranche of capital
goes.
**From:** the analysis in `CASO.md`, four transfer windows (2022/23–2025/26) of European
deal-level data plus FIFA's census 2016–2025.

Three recommendations, ranked by impact against effort. Anything that could not be defended with a
number is in "future exploration" at the end, not here.

---

## R1 — Buy into a persistent net seller in a mid-tier league, not into an elite club

- **Action.** Direct the next tranche at a club that has been a net seller in at least three of the
  last four seasons and plays in a mid-tier European league. The investment committee decides at
  the next quarterly meeting; the sporting side runs due diligence on the shortlist beforehand.
- **Evidence.** Of the 350 clubs present in all four seasons, **202 (58%) were net sellers in three
  or more, and 96 in all four** — against 61 expected if the role were random, so the role
  persists rather than rotating. The same measure names them: Ajax **+€267m** across four seasons,
  Salzburg **+€234m**, Lille **+€185m**, Genk **+€165m**, Feyenoord **+€157m**. At the other end,
  Chelsea (**−€800m**), Manchester United (−€672m) and Arsenal (−€637m) never finished a season as
  net sellers. Meanwhile the buying side has widened: **45% more clubs paid a fee in 2025 than in
  2018** (FIFA census), and the top-10 share of spending fell from 32.1% to 26.7%.
- **Expected impact.** A club of that profile clears €30–65m of net transfer income per season
  (Ajax and Salzburg average €59m over the four). **Assumption stated:** that the next four seasons
  resemble the last four in role stability — which is what H2 measures and nothing more.
- **How it is measured.** Net transfer balance of the invested club, per season, over three
  seasons. Success is a positive balance in at least two of three, with no single season below
  −€20m.
- **Risk.** Net transfer income is not profit: it says nothing about wages, amortisation or
  revenue. A club can sell well and still lose money. If the accounts contradict the transfer
  balance, this recommendation does not survive — see "data that would strengthen this" in
  `CASO.md` §6.
- **Effort.** Medium. It is a sourcing exercise on a shortlist that the data already produces.

---

## R2 — Cap prices against the market band for the role, and write into the mandate that age carries no premium

- **Action.** Add to the investment mandate that no acquisition may be priced above the market's
  band for a player of that role and level *on the grounds of age*. Applies to every club in the
  portfolio, from the next window.
- **Evidence.** The average fee for an 18–23 player divided by that of a 24–29 player sits at
  **1.07 in our data and 0.91 in FIFA's census for 2025** — around parity, and **it does not
  escalate in either source** over the period measured. The market does not pay more for youth as
  such. An earlier reading of ours said the ratio was falling; it did not survive checking against
  other specifications and was withdrawn, which is why this card claims parity and no more.
- **Expected impact.** Avoided overpayment. On the fund's young-player purchases, a 10% premium
  removed on a €40m signing is €4m per deal. **Assumption stated:** that the fund's clubs are
  currently paying such a premium — which we have not measured and should be checked against their
  own books before claiming a saving.
- **How it is measured.** Share of the fund's signings priced above the market band for their
  bracket, per window. Target: zero, with any exception minuted with its reason.
- **Risk.** A price band is a market average, and a genuinely exceptional player justifies leaving
  it. The rule must require a documented exception, not forbid one, or it will be ignored the first
  time a sporting director wants a player.
- **Effort.** Low. It is a policy line, not a project.

---

## R3 — Make each club pick its lane in the young-player market

- **Action.** For each club in the portfolio, state which of the two young-player markets it
  competes in — high-volume at low fees, or few deals at elite fees — and size its scouting and
  wage budget for that one. Decided per club at the annual planning cycle.
- **Evidence.** Inside the 18–23 bracket, deals of **€40m or more are 2.3% of transactions but take
  23.8% of the money**, while the **71% priced under €5m account for 15%**. The elite lane is
  dominated by a handful of buyers — Chelsea alone spent €688m across ten such deals, Liverpool
  €462m, PSG €430m — and seven of the top ten are English.
- **Expected impact.** Concentrating scouting on one lane rather than both. **Assumption stated:**
  that scouting cost scales with the breadth of the search rather than with the number of
  signings — plausible, and not measured here.
- **How it is measured.** Share of the club's signings that fall inside its declared lane, per
  window. Target above 80%.
- **Risk.** A club competing in the cheap lane cannot suddenly buy an elite young player when the
  chance appears; the discipline costs optionality. And the two lanes need different scouting
  networks, so switching later is expensive.
- **Effort.** Medium.

---

## Future exploration, deliberately not recommendations

- **The Saudi channel as an exit for players over 28.** Saudi clubs went from €9m in 22/23 to
  **€565m in 25/26**, 5.4% of all European-market spending across the four seasons. It could open
  a buyer for assets that used to depreciate unsold — but four seasons cannot separate a structural
  change from a spending cycle, and recommending a strategy on that basis would be exactly the kind
  of promise this case has spent five phases refusing to make.
- **Sell-on clauses.** FIFA reports that over half of permanent transfers now include one. If that
  holds for the clubs above, headline fees understate what a selling club eventually collects, and
  R1's impact figure is conservative. We do not have the clause data to say by how much.

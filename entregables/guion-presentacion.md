# Presentation script — three minutes

**Rehearse this out loud.** Reading it silently and knowing it are different things, and the
difference shows in the first fifteen seconds.

---

### 0:00 — The setup (~25s)

> "A multi-club investment fund asked a simple question: where do we put the next tranche — into a
> club that develops and sells players, or into one that buys them?
>
> Answering it meant testing two things the industry treats as obvious. That transfer money is
> concentrating in fewer and fewer clubs. And that young players now carry a growing premium.
>
> I wrote both down as a hypothesis, in the case file, before I opened the data. That matters for
> what comes next."

### 0:25 — The finding (~50s)

> "Both turned out to be wrong.
>
> The ten biggest buying clubs took 32% of European transfer spending in 2022/23. By 2025/26 it was
> 27%, and the number of clubs buying at all had gone from 339 to 382. The money is spreading out.
> That holds without English clubs in the sample, and FIFA's own census backs it over eight years:
> 45% more clubs paying transfer fees than in 2018.
>
> And youth: an eighteen-to-twenty-three-year-old costs almost exactly what a twenty-four to
> twenty-nine-year-old costs. A ratio of about one, in two independent sources, not rising."

### 1:15 — What *is* true (~35s)

> "What did hold up is who sells. Of 350 clubs present in all four seasons, 96 were net sellers in
> every single one. If the role were random you'd expect 61. Ajax banked €267m net over the period,
> Salzburg €234m. Chelsea and Manchester United never finished a season as net sellers — not once.
>
> Selling isn't a bad year. It's a position."

### 1:50 — The catch (~35s)

> "There's a wrinkle. Inside the young-player market, 2% of the deals take a quarter of the money.
> The other 71% are under five million euros and add up to 15%. Those are two different businesses
> wearing the same label, and a club drifting between them competes badly in both."

### 2:25 — The part I'm proudest of (~25s)

> "The dataset nearly gave me the opposite answer. Its history is rebuilt from players who are
> still in the database today, so coverage thins going backwards — and it thins by age. Taken at
> face value it says the youth share collapsed since 2010. That's not football, that's the scraper.
>
> I caught it in phase 2 by cross-checking against FIFA's census, cut the window to four clean
> seasons, and sourced every claim about a decade to the census instead."

### 2:50 — Close (~10s)

> "So the recommendation is to buy into a persistent seller in a mid-tier league, and to write into
> the mandate that age carries no premium. And the honest caveat: four seasons is not a trend, so
> anything I say about direction over time comes from FIFA, not from me."

---

## If they only ask one question, it will be one of these

- **"Four seasons isn't much."** — Correct, and that is why no trend claim rests on it. The
  decade comes from FIFA's census.
- **"Isn't this just the Premier League?"** — Checked. Without English buyers the concentration
  still falls, 30.6% to 24.0%.
- **"Most clubs being net sellers is just arithmetic."** — The count is; the persistence isn't.
  Against a null model, both tails are over-represented.
